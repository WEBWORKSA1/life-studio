Feeling stuck is rarely a lack of options. It's usually a lack of a process for choosing between them. Designers solve messy problems with a repeatable loop — and the same loop works remarkably well on a life.

The idea of applying design thinking to life was popularised by Stanford's Bill Burnett and Dave Evans in Designing Your Life. Below is our streamlined, action-first version you can complete in a weekend.

Step 1 — Assess: know where you actually are

You can't navigate without a starting point. Spend 10 minutes on the Wheel of Life and rate eight areas from 1–10. Don't overthink it; your first instinct is usually accurate.

  • Circle your two lowest scores. These drain energy from everything else.
  • Circle your highest. This is a strength you can lean on.
  • Write one sentence per area: "It's a 4 because…"

Step 2 — Explore: generate more options than feel comfortable

Most people consider one or two paths and then agonise. Designers generate many. Try the three lives exercise: sketch three completely different five-year plans.

  1. Life A: the path you're on, done well.
  2. Life B: what you'd do if Life A suddenly disappeared.
  3. Life C: what you'd do if money and opinions didn't matter.

For each, rate how excited you feel, how confident you are, and whether it's coherent with your values. You'll often find elements of B and C you can borrow today.

Step 3 — Prototype: test before you leap

Big decisions feel risky because we treat them as irreversible. Make them smaller. A prototype is any cheap, fast way to experience a slice of an option:

  • Conversations: talk to three people already living the life you're curious about.
  • Experiences: shadow someone for a day, take a weekend course, do one freelance project.
  • Numbers: run your finances through the financial freedom calculator to see what a change really costs.
Don't ask "Is this the right path?" Ask "What's the smallest way to find out?"

Step 4 — Commit: pick one area and one goal

Trying to fix everything at once is the most common reason redesigns fail. Choose one area for the next 90 days and write it as a SMART goal in the goal planner. Then add an implementation intention — "If it's 7am on a weekday, then I walk for 20 minutes." Research by psychologist Peter Gollwitzer and colleagues has repeatedly found that if-then planning improves follow-through.

Step 5 — Review: iterate every week

Block 20 minutes each Sunday. Ask three questions: What worked? What didn't? What will I change? Re-take the Wheel of Life every 90 days and watch the shape of your life change.

Your weekend plan: Saturday morning — Steps 1–2. Saturday afternoon — book two prototype conversations. Sunday — Step 4 and schedule your first weekly review.
