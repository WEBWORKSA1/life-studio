Burnout isn't a personal weakness. It's a signal that demands and resources have been out of balance for too long.

What burnout is

The World Health Organization's ICD-11 describes burnout as an occupational phenomenon resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed, characterised by three dimensions: exhaustion, increased mental distance or cynicism toward one's job, and reduced professional efficacy.

Warning signs

  • Tiredness that sleep doesn't fix
  • Dreading work you used to enjoy
  • Irritability, detachment or cynicism
  • Trouble concentrating; small tasks feel huge
  • Getting sick more often; headaches or poor sleep

Some of these overlap with depression and other health conditions. If symptoms are severe or persistent, please see a doctor or licensed mental-health professional.

A 4-phase recovery plan

1. Stop the bleeding (week 1–2)

Identify what can be paused, delegated or dropped. Protect sleep. Tell one trusted person how you're really doing.

2. Restore the basics (weeks 2–6)

Consistent sleep and wake times, daily movement, regular meals, time outdoors, and one activity purely for enjoyment each week.

3. Fix the causes (weeks 4–12)

Common drivers include workload, lack of control, insufficient reward, poor community, unfairness and values conflict. Pick the biggest one and address it directly — a conversation with your manager, clearer boundaries, or a role change.

4. Prevent relapse

Re-take the Wheel of Life monthly. Watch for early signs and act while they're small.

A burnout-recovery or career coach can help with boundary-setting and workplace conversations. Get matched free. If you're in crisis, contact local emergency services or a crisis line right away.
